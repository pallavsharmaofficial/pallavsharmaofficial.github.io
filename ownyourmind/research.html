OwnYourMind — research and philosophy

"Know the Self as the lord of the chariot, the body as the chariot, the intellect as the charioteer and the mind as the reins. The senses are the horses." — Katha Upanishad 1.3.3–4

This document is why the app is shaped the way it is. Each feature is matched to a practice that comes either from the contemplative traditions or from measured psychology, and ideally from both.

1. The problem: most of the day is spent on autopilot

  • Mind-wandering is the default. Killingsworth & Gilbert (2010, Science) pinged 2,250 people at random moments through an iPhone app. In 46.9 % of samples, people were thinking about something other than what they were doing. People were less happy while their minds wandered, and the time-lag analysis suggested the wandering came first and the unhappiness followed. Caveat: the study is observational, and the sample was self-selected iPhone owners.
  • Habits run without being chosen. The cue → routine → reward loop does its work below awareness. Judson Brewer's research reframes worry, craving and scrolling as reward-based learning loops. His central claim is that a habit changes sustainably only when its reward value is updated, and that updating happens when we look clearly, with curiosity, at what the behaviour actually gives us.
  • Two systems. Kahneman's System 1 (fast, automatic) and System 2 (slow, deliberate) map onto the tradition's manas (the reactive mind) and buddhi (the discerning intellect). The aim is not to kill System 1. It is to put a charioteer back in the seat.

What this means for the app: you can't watch the autopilot from inside the autopilot. Something outside the loop has to interrupt it at random, unchosen moments. A phone that is always in your pocket can do exactly that.

2. The witness (Sakshi): the voice the app tries to grow

Every tradition that studies the mind arrives at the same move: step back from the content of experience and notice the one who is aware of it.

Tradition Name for it Practice
Upanishads / Advaita Sakshi, the witness neti neti; Ramana Maharshi's "Who am I?" self-inquiry
Bhagavad Gita 6.5 uddhared ātmanātmānam — "lift the self by the Self" abhyasa (practice) and vairagya (non-clinging)
Buddhism sati (mindfulness), sampajañña (clear knowing) Satipaṭṭhāna; Mahasi noting ("thinking… hearing…")
Stoicism prosochē, attention to oneself Marcus Aurelius' evening notes; Seneca's nightly review
Gurdjieff self-remembering divided attention: "I am here, doing this"
Logotherapy the space between stimulus and response choosing one's attitude (Frankl)
Internal Family Systems Self vs. parts unblending: "a part of me is angry"

The app's Inner Voice speaks for the witness, never for the parts. It doesn't scold, motivate or judge. It asks, names and reflects, then hands the choice back to you.

3. Practical mechanisms with evidence

Mechanism Evidence How the app uses it
Random experience sampling The ESM/DES method used by Killingsworth & Gilbert and by Hurlburt's Descriptive Experience Sampling. Being asked "what was in your mind just now?" at an unchosen moment is the most accurate window into the autopilot we have. Hurlburt found that questionnaires report inner speech 2–4× more often than random samples do, so we know ourselves worse than we think. Witness pings: random times during waking hours, never on the hour, so they can't become habitual. Each ping leads to a 10-second check-in: Where was attention? What was the mind doing? Name the feeling. The body?
Affect labeling Lieberman et al. (2007): putting a feeling into words reduced amygdala response and increased right ventrolateral prefrontal activity. Every check-in asks you to name the feeling from a short vocabulary. The RAIN practice repeats the move.
Self-distancing self-talk Kross et al. (2014, JPSP, six studies): referring to yourself by name or in the second or third person lowered distress and improved performance under stress. The Inner Voice talks to you by your name, in the second person ("Pallav, what is the body saying?"). It is the witness speaking to the person.
Implementation intentions Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006): d ≈ 0.65 across 94 tests. The 2024 update (642 tests) is smaller after bias correction (d ≈ 0.15–0.35) but still positive. Morning Sankalpa with an if–then plan: "If I reach for my phone without deciding to, then I will take one breath and ask who is reaching."
Curiosity over willpower Brewer's habit-loop work: noticing what a behaviour really feels like updates its reward value. Catch the autopilot: log the cue, the urge's strength, what you chose, and how it actually felt. The Map then shows you your own loops.
Urge surfing Marlatt's relapse-prevention work: urges rise, peak and pass like waves, usually within minutes. The Urge surf practice: a breathing wave you ride until the urge falls.
Just-in-time support Nahum-Shani et al. (2017) define JITAIs: the right support at the right moment. Evidence that this works for mindfulness is still early, mostly feasibility trials. Pings and the Inner Voice adapt to your last state. Two autopilot answers in a row bring a shorter, gentler prompt and a breath.
Evening review The Stoic and Pythagorean nightly review, and Ignatius' Examen. The evening review asks: when was I awake? when was I asleep at the wheel? what would the witness do differently tomorrow?

Honest limits. No app makes anyone aware. Awareness is the one thing a notification can't do for you. It can only knock. Large app-based mindfulness trials show modest average effects, and most users drop off within weeks. The design therefore favours many tiny moments over long sessions: a 10-second check-in, a 60-second pause, one breath. The aim is for the knock to become internal. After weeks of random pings, people start "pinging themselves". That is the point where the app's voice has become yours.

4. The map: Pancha Kosha, the five sheaths

The Taittiriya Upanishad describes the self as covered by five sheaths, from the outermost to the innermost. The app's Inner Map uses them as layers of awareness:

  1. Annamaya, the body: did you notice the body? (body check-ins, posture, tension)
  2. Pranamaya, breath and energy: breath practices, energy levels
  3. Manomaya, the reactive mind: mind activity, feelings, urges, autopilot catches
  4. Vijnanamaya, discernment: intentions kept, choices made in the gap, reviews
  5. Anandamaya, bliss, the closest sheath to the Self: moments of being fully here, self-inquiry

Each layer lights up as you practise it. Alongside the layers, the Map shows the hours of the day your autopilot is strongest, your most frequent mind activities and feelings, and the loops you've caught (cue → choice). It is a map of where you go when you are not there.

5. Design principles

  1. Ask, don't tell. The voice asks questions, and the answers belong to you.
  2. Short beats long. Every interaction finishes in under a minute unless you choose a practice.
  3. No shame. "Autopilot" is data, not failure. Catching it is awareness.
  4. Private. Everything stays on the device. No account, no server.
  5. Real world. Images of real places: forests, mountains, water, sky. They pull attention out of the screen.
  6. Fade the app. The best outcome is needing it less: fewer pings, more self-catches.
